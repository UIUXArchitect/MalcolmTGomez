Approach / Principles / Problem Solving

How I Think.

A few principles that keep showing up in the way I approach strategy, partnerships and experience design.

01 — Find the actual problem.
The thing people initially ask for and the thing they actually need are often different.
02 — Start with people, not the product.
I want to understand behavior, motivation and friction before deciding what should be built, marketed or changed.
03 — Make complexity legible.
Strategy becomes useful when people can understand it, repeat it and act on it.
04 — Look outside the category.
Good answers don't always come from the industry you're working in. I like borrowing ideas from technology, entertainment, retail, culture and anywhere else the problem has already been solved differently.
05 — Strategy has to survive contact with reality.
A good idea still has to work with budgets, timelines, customers, partners and the people responsible for executing it.
06 — Leave room for taste.
Data can tell you a lot. It can't always tell you what will feel distinctive, memorable or worth caring about.
07 — Understand who has to say yes.
A good idea can still fail if it doesn't make sense to the person making the decision. I try to understand what matters to them, what they're accountable for and what would make the decision easier.
08 — Build the system, not just the idea.
I like the creative part of solving problems, but I'm just as interested in what happens afterward. Who owns it? What happens next? What breaks when it grows? The details often determine whether an idea works.
09 — Know what success actually looks like.
More attention, more activity and more meetings don't always mean progress. Before deciding what to do, I want to understand what we're trying to change and what evidence would tell us we're getting there.

Film Room
Film / Theory / History / Criticism

The artform that changed the way I see the world.

I always loved movies. Studying them changed me.

Film was my first love long before I studied Film at the University of North Texas. What I didn't expect was how studying film theory, history and criticism would open my mind. I started paying attention to the world around a story: who was telling it, whose perspective was missing, the history behind it, and why two people could watch the same scene and come away with different interpretations.

That education was happening as I was becoming an adult. It taught me that being observant isn't the same as understanding—and that the first interpretation is rarely the only one worth considering.

What Studying Film Gave Me

01 — Look beyond the frame.
Every story is a set of choices. Perspective, editing, sound and what gets left out all shape what we believe we're seeing.
02 — Context changes meaning.
History and culture don't sit outside a film. They're part of how it is made, received and remembered.
03 — Sit with complexity.
Films let me encounter lives and perspectives different from my own. They taught me to resist easy judgments and pay closer attention.
04 — Make a case for what you see.
Criticism isn't just having an opinion. It's noticing patterns, building an interpretation and being willing to revise it when the evidence changes.

Where It Shows Up in My Work

Those habits didn't disappear when I moved into technology, recruiting, strategy or partnerships. When I look at a market, a brand or a live experience, I still ask questions that feel familiar from the screening room: Who is the audience? Whose perspective are we missing? What's the story we're telling—and what are we leaving out?

Film didn't give me a business playbook. It gave me a way to look closer, consider another perspective and turn an observation into a defensible point of view. That's useful anywhere the work depends on understanding people.

Films That Stay With Me

Twelve films I return to for different reasons. Each changed what I notice about people, power, place or the way stories are told.

The Florida Project
A child's world in full color, with an adult reality always just outside the frame. It reminds me how much perspective determines what a story reveals.
4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days
A lesson in how systems shape private lives. Its restraint makes the tension feel unbearable without telling the audience what to think.
American Beauty
The polished surface of suburban life hides a much messier reality. A reminder to question appearances and the stories people tell about themselves.
Fish Tank
A character who resists easy labels, and a camera willing to stay close enough to notice. Understanding someone sometimes requires patience rather than judgment.
No Country for Old Men
The world doesn't always reward good intentions or deliver neat explanations. Its refusal to provide easy certainty is what makes it linger.
One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest
Power, conformity and the cost of resisting both. It's impossible to watch without thinking about the institutions that decide what counts as normal.
The Shawshank Redemption
A film about dignity, friendship and hope as something practiced rather than promised. Its emotional power comes from the details, not just the ending.
Hereditary
Horror that lives inside a family long before anything supernatural happens. The performances, spaces and silences make grief feel almost physical.
A Ghost Story
How patience, silence and time can communicate more than explanation. It trusts the audience to stay with an emotion instead of rushing past it.
Parasite
How architecture, space and small details can carry an argument about class. The setting isn't background; it's part of the storytelling.
Beasts of the Southern Wild
A child's imagination becomes a way of understanding a threatened world. There's something extraordinary about its mix of vulnerability, community and wonder.
Chop Shop (2007)
The rhythms of everyday life and a place that feels inhabited rather than staged. It finds drama and dignity in what most stories overlook.

Behind the Camera

Studying film taught me to analyze creative choices. Directing taught me what it takes to make them. I've directed theater, including a production of Dreamgirls, and my work with the Denton Black Film Festival keeps me connected to stories and storytellers. I know the distance between an idea on paper and the collaboration required to bring it to life.

Visual Instincts

I'm drawn to films that feel observed rather than manufactured. Small casts. Naturalistic performances. Long takes that allow a moment to unfold without rushing the audience toward the next one. Minimal cutting, grain, imperfect textures and spaces that feel genuinely inhabited.

I pay attention to the ordinary rituals of life. The way someone moves through a room, what they surround themselves with, the silence between two people or the smallest change in someone's expression. Those details can reveal class, identity, relationships and emotional histories long before a character says a word.

I've always appreciated filmmakers who trust their audience. Who understand that framing, sound, pacing and what happens outside the frame can be just as important as dialogue. Sometimes what a film chooses not to show is what makes it linger.

That doesn't mean everything has to be understated or realistic. I love bold creative choices when they serve a purpose. But I'm especially drawn to films that give me room to observe, interpret and discover something for myself.

And sometimes, I just love a great movie.
